UNIT 2 · NATURAL RESOURCES

Natural Resources: use, pressure, degradation and conservation.

Natural resources are the goods and services supplied by nature that support life and human society. This unit studies renewable and non-renewable resources and then examines land, forest, water and energy resources in detail, including the environmental and social problems created by overuse.

01 · NATURAL RESOURCES

What makes something a natural resource?

Natural resources are the variety of goods and services provided by nature that are necessary for day-to-day life. They include living or biotic resources such as plants, animals and microorganisms, and non-living or abiotic resources such as air, water, soil, minerals, climate and solar energy. They support physiological needs such as food and water, but also social, economic and cultural needs at both individual and community levels.

Renewable resources

Renewable resources are replenished through comparatively rapid natural cycles. Biological resources such as crops, forests and animals may increase through reproduction and growth. Water and soil are also commonly classified as renewable because they participate in natural cycles, but they can still become depleted or degraded if use is faster than recovery. Solar energy is treated as renewable because its stock is effectively inexhaustible on the human time scale.

The study material also emphasizes that some renewable materials can be recovered or recycled after use. Examples include wood and wood products, pulp products, natural rubber, fibres such as cotton, jute, wool and silk, and leather. This helps students understand that renewability can involve biological regeneration, natural cycling and, for some materials, repeated reuse.

Non-renewable resources

Non-renewable resources are available in limited quantities and cannot be replenished by natural processes within a useful human time scale. Examples include fossil fuels such as coal and petroleum; metals such as iron, copper, gold, silver, lead and zinc; and mineral deposits such as carbonates, phosphates and nitrates.

Recyclable non-renewables

Some non-energy mineral resources can be collected after use and recycled. Examples include metals obtained from ores such as aluminium and copper, and certain mineral materials.

Non-recyclable non-renewables

Fossil fuels and uranium are consumed as energy sources and cannot be recycled back into their original energy resource after use.

Unequal consumption of resources

A major problem associated with natural resources is unequal consumption. The reference material points out that resource use per person is much higher in developed countries, while developing countries may also exert strong pressure because of larger populations. This means environmental pressure depends both on population and on per-capita consumption. High energy use, high material consumption and high waste generation all increase the ecological burden.

02 · LAND RESOURCES

Land use, land-cover change and land degradation

Land is a basic resource on which agriculture, forests, settlements, roads, industries and natural habitats depend. Land cover describes what physically covers the surface—forest, grass, crops, water, buildings or bare soil. Land use describes how people use that land—for farming, housing, industry, transport, conservation and other purposes. A change in land use often produces a change in land cover; for example, converting forest into agricultural fields removes natural vegetation and changes soil, runoff and habitat conditions.

Land degradation

Land degradation is a decline in the quality and productive capacity of land. It can involve loss of soil fertility and nutrients, reduction of vegetation cover, changes in soil structure, contamination, salinization and increased erosion. Human activities such as deforestation, overgrazing, mining, poorly managed irrigation, intensive cultivation and construction can accelerate degradation.

Degraded land becomes less capable of supporting crops, grazing or natural vegetation. Once plant cover is reduced, rainfall can strike the soil directly and runoff increases. In dry areas the combined effect of vegetation loss, repeated cultivation, overgrazing and poor water management can push land toward desertification.

Soil erosion

Soil erosion is the removal of the upper layer of soil by agents such as flowing water and wind. The topsoil contains much of the organic matter and plant-available nutrients, so its loss reduces agricultural productivity. Vegetation protects soil by intercepting rainfall, binding particles with roots and reducing runoff. Removing vegetation therefore makes erosion more severe, particularly on slopes.

Vegetation loss→soil exposed→greater runoff/wind action→topsoil loss→lower fertility

Desertification

Desertification is land degradation in dry regions in which productive land progressively loses vegetation and soil quality and becomes increasingly desert-like. Repeated drought can contribute, but human pressures such as overgrazing, excessive cultivation, deforestation and poor water management can intensify the process. Conservation therefore requires maintaining vegetation, controlling erosion, using water carefully and matching land use to the land's ecological capacity.

03 · FOREST RESOURCES

Functions, uses, over-exploitation and deforestation

A forest is a biological community dominated by trees, shrubs and other woody vegetation. Forests perform important ecological and economic functions. They provide habitat for plants and animals, recycle rainwater, influence water quality, moderate temperature and weather, reduce erosion and help remove pollutants from air.

Commercial uses

Wood is used as fuel and as raw material for pulp, paper, furniture and construction. Forests also provide gums, dyes, resins, medicines, honey and many other useful products.

Ecological uses

Forests produce oxygen, absorb carbon dioxide during photosynthesis, support wildlife, moderate air and noise pollution, influence soil condition and help regulate water movement.

Over-exploitation of forests

Population growth and increasing demand for medicine, shelter, wood and fuel can intensify extraction from forests. Expansion of agriculture, growing demand for wood resources and repeated use of forest land for other purposes increase pressure on forest ecosystems.

Deforestation

Deforestation is the removal or destruction of forest cover through natural or human activities. Development projects can destroy forest directly or submerge it under reservoirs. Mining reduces forest area. Industries require wood for boxes, furniture and paper. Rural and tribal communities may depend on fuelwood. Shifting cultivation, forest fires and overgrazing are additional pressures mentioned in the reference material.

Consequences of deforestation

The effects extend well beyond loss of trees. Deforestation can cause economic loss, biodiversity decline and destruction of habitats. Reduced vegetation can intensify global warming, disturb weather patterns, degrade soil and accelerate erosion. On slopes it can increase the risk of mass movement and landslides, and in catchments it may increase the frequency and severity of floods.

Timber extraction

Timber refers to wood used for engineering and construction purposes such as buildings and furniture. Poorly managed logging degrades forests and fragments remaining habitat. The reference notes effects such as loss of biodiversity, climatic changes, intensified flooding in upstream watersheds, soil erosion on slopes and exploitation of tribal communities by contractors.

Mining and forests

Mining is the extraction of mineral resources and fossil fuels such as coal from the earth. Surface mining removes shallow deposits, while underground mining reaches deeper deposits. Mining can pollute soil, water and air, destroy natural habitat, generate noise, reduce forest area and increase landslide risk. Excavated trenches may collect water, and contaminated water can affect groundwater.

Dams and forests

Large reservoirs can submerge forest and farmland and transform river ecosystems. The reference material lists loss of forest area, destruction of wildlife and aquatic life, spread of water-borne disease, and waterlogging and salinity as important environmental concerns. At the same time dams may provide water supply, irrigation, hydropower and flood regulation.

Impact on tribal and forest-dependent populations

For many tribal communities, forests provide food, fuel, medicines, grazing, employment, cultural identity and traditional living space. Large dams and development projects may cause displacement and cultural change. The material stresses that displacement can affect people physically, socially and psychologically, and that resettlement and compensation may be inadequate when livelihoods and community relationships are not restored.

04 · WATER RESOURCES

Uses, over-exploitation, floods, drought and conflicts

Water is essential for all forms of life. In addition to drinking and domestic use, large quantities are needed for irrigation, industry, recreation and environmental functions. Surface water occurs in rivers, lakes, ponds and reservoirs, while groundwater is stored in soil and rock formations below the surface.

Effects of over-utilization of groundwater

When groundwater extraction is greater than recharge, groundwater levels decline. Insufficient rainfall and construction that seals the soil surface can reduce recharge further. As aquifer sediments lose water they may compact, causing ground subsidence. This can damage buildings, fracture pipes and disturb canals. Persistent over-pumping also lowers the water table and can dry up dug wells and borewells.

The source also connects groundwater decline with water-quality problems. In agricultural regions, pollutants such as nitrates can reach groundwater, making water less suitable for drinking. Thus the problem is not only how much groundwater remains, but also whether its quality is safe.

Floods

A flood occurs when the flow of water exceeds the carrying capacity of a river channel and spreads over surrounding land. Heavy rainfall, melting snow and sudden releases from dams can cause floods. A reduction in channel capacity can worsen the situation, while deforestation, mining and overgrazing may increase runoff from rainfall.

Floods may submerge settlements and cultivated land and can cause very severe social and economic damage. Management measures described in the reference include reservoir management, channel management, forecasting and flood warning, and reducing runoff by improving infiltration through afforestation in catchment areas.

Drought

Drought is a condition of water scarcity associated with inadequate rainfall, delayed rainfall or excessive groundwater withdrawal. It becomes more severe when annual rainfall is below normal while evaporation and demand remain high. High population pressure and water-intensive cropping patterns can intensify drought.

The effects include shortage of drinking water, hunger and malnutrition, reduced water quality, widespread crop failure and stress on livestock. Severe drought can accelerate desertification, reduce the supply of raw materials to agro-based industries and increase degradation of natural resources.

Drought management

The study material recommends rainwater harvesting, reservoirs and recharge measures, modern irrigation methods such as drip irrigation, afforestation, indigenous knowledge, crop mixing and dry farming. The central principle is to reduce demand while increasing the amount of rainwater retained in the landscape.

Conflicts over water

Unequal distribution of water leads to interstate and international disputes. Examples listed in the study material include the Cauvery dispute between Karnataka and Tamil Nadu and the Krishna water-sharing issue involving Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh. International examples include the Indus between India and Pakistan and the Colorado River between the United States and Mexico.

Dams: benefits and problems

Dams can ensure year-round water supply for domestic use, agriculture and industry and can support hydropower generation. However, large dams may submerge farmland and forests, cause siltation, waterlogging and salinity, alter aquatic ecosystems, fragment rivers and create serious resettlement and rehabilitation problems. The source therefore notes that smaller dams may sometimes reduce environmental impact compared with very large projects.

Water-resource principle: rainfall, catchment vegetation, surface flow, groundwater recharge, agriculture, settlements, industries and ecosystems are interconnected. Managing only one part of the system can shift the problem somewhere else.
05 · ENERGY RESOURCES

Growing needs, unequal consumption and alternate energy

Energy is required for households, transport, agriculture, industry, communication and almost every modern economic activity. The study material highlights the unequal global distribution of energy consumption: a relatively small share of the world's population in highly developed countries consumes a disproportionately large share of available energy. This reinforces the idea that environmental pressure depends not only on population but also on lifestyle and per-capita energy demand.

Renewable and non-renewable energy

Renewable or non-conventional energy sources can be regenerated or are continuously available on the human time scale. Non-renewable or conventional energy resources are finite and include fossil fuels and nuclear fuels. Growing energy demand makes conservation and diversification of energy sources increasingly important.

Renewable energy sources

Solar energy

The Sun produces enormous energy through nuclear fusion. Solar energy can be collected and used in several ways. Photovoltaic cells convert sunlight directly to electricity, while solar thermal devices use solar radiation for heating.

Wind energy

Wind turbines convert the kinetic energy of moving air into mechanical rotation and then electrical energy. Groups of turbines installed together form wind farms.

Tidal / ocean energy

Tidal systems use periodic seawater movement caused mainly by gravitational interactions involving the Moon and Sun. Suitable coastal locations can convert this movement to electricity.

Geothermal energy

Geothermal systems use heat from within the Earth. Where geological conditions are suitable, this heat can be used directly or to generate electricity.

Biomass and biogas

Organic material from plants, animals and wastes can be used as an energy source. Anaerobic digestion of organic waste produces methane-rich biogas that can be used as fuel.

Hydropower

Flowing or falling water can drive turbines to generate electricity. It is renewable, but large hydroelectric projects can create ecological and social impacts through reservoirs and river modification.

Non-renewable energy sources

Coal, petroleum and natural gas are fossil fuels formed over geological time. They are consumed much faster than they are naturally formed. Their use supplies large amounts of energy but contributes to depletion, air pollution and greenhouse-gas emissions. Nuclear power uses finite fuels such as uranium and therefore also belongs to the non-renewable category.

Why alternate energy sources matter

Alternate energy sources reduce dependence on finite fossil fuels and can lower pollution during operation. However, no technology is completely impact-free. Sustainable energy planning combines renewable generation with energy conservation, efficient devices, careful siting, storage and responsible management of land and materials.
