06 · BOOLEAN ALGEBRA

Boolean Algebra — the language used to simplify digital logic

Digital circuits work with two logical states: 0 and 1. Boolean algebra gives us a mathematical way to describe those states and the operations performed by logic gates. Instead of drawing a large circuit every time, we can write the circuit as a Boolean expression, manipulate that expression using Boolean laws, and then build an equivalent but simpler circuit.

Logic inputs 0 / 1→Boolean expression→Apply Boolean laws→Simpler expression→Simpler logic circuit
00 · BEFORE THE LAWS

What are Boolean laws?

Boolean laws are rules that tell us when one logical expression can be replaced by another without changing the output. They are similar in purpose to the rules used when simplifying ordinary algebra, but Boolean algebra operates only on logical values 0 and 1.

For example, the expression A + A·B looks as though it needs an AND operation followed by an OR operation. The Absorption Law tells us that:

A + A·B = A

Both expressions give exactly the same output for every possible value of A and B. Therefore the larger expression may be replaced by the much simpler expression A.

Why do we need the laws?

A Boolean expression obtained from a truth table or circuit may contain unnecessary terms and variables. The laws let us remove this redundancy while preserving exactly the same logical behaviour.

What does simplification achieve?

A simpler expression can require fewer gates, fewer gate inputs and fewer interconnections. The logical function remains unchanged, but its implementation becomes easier to understand and build.

How are laws connected to logic gates?

Boolean operators correspond directly to gates: · means AND, + means OR, and a complement such as A̅ means NOT. Simplifying the equation therefore simplifies the gate network.

Why prove the laws?

We should not simply assume that two expressions are equivalent. A truth table evaluates both sides for every possible input. If LHS and RHS always match, the replacement is valid.

How does Boolean algebra help a circuit?

Example:

F = A·B + A·B̅

Factor A using the distributive law:

F = A(B + B̅)

From the Complement Law, B + B̅ = 1. Therefore:

F = A·1 = A

The original expression describes two AND terms feeding an OR operation. After simplification, the entire function is simply F = A. The Boolean laws have shown that the extra logic is unnecessary.

How is this linked to Karnaugh maps?

Boolean algebra and a Karnaugh map are not unrelated topics. Both are methods for simplifying the same Boolean function. Boolean algebra performs the simplification symbolically using laws. A K-map arranges the truth-table combinations so that terms which differ in only one variable become adjacent, allowing the same simplification to be seen visually.

See the connection:

A̅B̅ + A̅B = A̅(B̅ + B) = A̅

Algebraically, B̅ + B = 1, so B disappears. In a K-map, these two minterms occupy adjacent cells because only B changes. Grouping the two cells removes B and leaves A̅. A K-map is therefore a visual way of applying the same simplification principle.

Truth table→Boolean function→Boolean lawsORK-map grouping→Minimized expression→Logic circuit
Important idea: Boolean laws explain why simplification is valid. K-maps make many of those simplifications easier to see, especially as the number of terms increases. Algebraic manipulation can become tedious for larger expressions, which is one reason K-maps are useful.

How will the laws be proved?

A Boolean law states that two expressions are equivalent for every possible input. For each law below, calculate the LHS and RHS for every input combination. If the two output columns are identical in every row, the law is proved.

Write the law→List all inputs→Calculate LHS→Calculate RHS→Compare
How to read the proofs: LHS means Left-Hand Side and RHS means Right-Hand Side. The green ✓ shows that both sides produce exactly the same output.

02 · ORDER AND REDUNDANCY

Commutative and Absorption Laws

Commutative Law

A+B=B+A    |    AB=BA

The order of the inputs does not matter for AND or OR. Swapping the two inputs of the gate leaves the output unchanged.

Absorption Law

A+AB=A    |    A(A+B)=A

In A+AB, whenever AB becomes 1, A is already 1. The term AB therefore adds no new condition and is absorbed by A. Similarly, in A(A+B), A already determines when the output can be 1, so the extra B does not change the result.

04 · PRINCIPLE OF DUALITY

Principle of Duality

The Principle of Duality is a useful property of Boolean algebra. It says that Boolean laws naturally occur in pairs. If a Boolean identity is valid, another valid identity can be obtained by interchanging the OR and AND operations and, at the same time, interchanging the constants 0 and 1.

The variables themselves do not change. A remains A and A̅ remains A̅. Duality therefore does not mean taking the complement of the whole expression. It means constructing the corresponding dual form of the Boolean identity.

What changes when we form the dual?

+ ↔ ·

Every OR operation is replaced by AND, and every AND operation is replaced by OR.

0 ↔ 1

Every Boolean constant 0 is replaced by 1, and every Boolean constant 1 is replaced by 0.

Example — Identity Law

Start with the valid Boolean identity:

A + 0 = A

Replace OR by AND and 0 by 1. The variable A remains unchanged:

A · 1 = A

The result is the other form of the Identity Law. Both identities are valid.

More examples of dual pairs

Boolean identityIts dual
A + 0 = AA · 1 = A
A + 1 = 1A · 0 = 0
A + A = AA · A = A
A + A̅ = 1A · A̅ = 0
A + BC = (A+B)(A+C)A(B+C) = AB+AC

Why is the Principle of Duality useful?

Many Boolean laws have an OR form and a corresponding AND form. Duality explains why these paired forms appear. Once one identity is known, its dual can be written directly by applying the interchange rules. This makes it easier to recognize relationships among Boolean laws and to work with equivalent forms during simplification.

Important distinction: Duality and complementation are different operations. For example, the dual of A+0=A is A·1=A. We did not complement A. De Morgan’s theorems, on the other hand, specifically deal with the complement of an expression.
05 · DE MORGAN'S THEOREMS

De Morgan’s Theorems

De Morgan’s theorems explain how the complement of an entire Boolean expression can be rewritten. The complement bar over a complete expression does not simply disappear. When it is moved from the complete expression to the individual variables, the Boolean operation also changes.

There are two theorems. One begins with an OR expression and the other begins with an AND expression.

First Theorem — Complement of an OR expression

(A+B)̅ = A̅·B̅

The expression A+B is 1 whenever at least one input is 1. Therefore its complement (A+B)̅ can be 1 only when both A and B are 0. When A=0 and B=0, their complements A̅ and B̅ are both 1, so A̅·B̅ is also 1. This is why the complement of OR becomes the AND of the complemented inputs.

Second Theorem — Complement of an AND expression

(A·B)̅ = A̅+B̅

The expression A·B is 1 only when both inputs are 1. Its complement is therefore 0 only in that one case and is 1 whenever A=0 or B=0. Whenever A=0, A̅=1; whenever B=0, B̅=1. Hence the same condition is represented by A̅+B̅. This is why the complement of AND becomes the OR of the complemented inputs.

Extension to more than two variables

The same idea applies to expressions containing three or more variables.

(A+B+C)̅=A̅B̅C̅

The complemented OR expression is 1 only when A, B and C are all 0. Therefore all three individual complements must be 1 together.

(ABC)̅=A̅+B̅+C̅

The complemented AND expression is 1 whenever at least one of A, B or C is 0. The OR of the individual complements represents exactly that condition.

Why De Morgan’s theorems are useful

They allow complemented expressions to be rewritten into equivalent forms. This is important when simplifying Boolean expressions and when changing the type of gates used to implement a function. In particular, they show the relationship between AND/OR logic and NAND/NOR logic.

NAND connection: A NAND operation is the complement of AND:

(AB)̅=A̅+B̅

So a NAND function can also be viewed as an OR operation whose inputs are complemented.

NOR connection: A NOR operation is the complement of OR:

(A+B)̅=A̅B̅

So a NOR function can also be viewed as an AND operation whose inputs are complemented.

Boolean simplification step by step

F=AB+A B̅

Step 1 — Factor A using the Distributive Law:

F=A(B+B̅)

Step 2 — Complement Law: B+B̅=1

F=A·1

Step 3 — Identity Law:

F=A

The original two product terms and OR operation reduce to a single signal A.

(A+B)(A+C)

Expanding and then applying Idempotent and Absorption laws gives:

AA+AC+AB+BC → A+AC+AB+BC → A+AB+BC → A+BC

Each transformation preserves the function while removing unnecessary terms.
