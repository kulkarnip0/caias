UNIT I · ECOSYSTEM

Understand an ecosystem as a working system.

An ecosystem is not simply a list of plants and animals. It is a functional system in which living organisms interact with one another and with light, water, air, soil, temperature and nutrients. Energy enters, organisms feed, matter is recycled and each component affects the others.

01 · ECOLOGY — START HERE

Ecology asks: how does life interact with its surroundings?

The word ecology was introduced by the German biologist Ernst Haeckel in 1869. It comes from the Greek words oikos, meaning house, habitat or place of living, and logos, meaning study. Ecology therefore literally suggests the study of organisms in their “home”.

In environmental science, ecology is the study of interactions among organisms, groups of organisms and their environment. The environment contains both living and non-living parts. A deer depends on grass for food, water for drinking, oxygen from the atmosphere and a suitable temperature range. None of these relationships can be understood by studying the deer alone.

ABIOTIC · NON-LIVING

Physical and chemical surroundings

Light, water, wind, soil nutrients, heat, solar radiation, air, temperature, pH and salinity are examples. These factors determine where organisms can survive and how productive an ecosystem can be.

BIOTIC · LIVING

Living organisms

Plants, animals and microorganisms form the biotic part. They interact through feeding, competition, decomposition and many other relationships.

02 · WHAT IS AN ECOSYSTEM?

The basic functional unit of ecology.

The term ecosystem was proposed by A. G. Tansley in 1935. An ecosystem includes the biological community living in a particular place together with the physical and chemical factors that form its non-living environment.

In simple words, an ecosystem is a group of organisms interacting among themselves and with their environment while exchanging energy and matter. A pond, forest, desert, estuary and ocean are all ecosystems, even though their size, organisms and environmental conditions are very different.

03 · STRUCTURE OF AN ECOSYSTEM

Structure means: what components are present?

The structure of an ecosystem refers to its different components and how they are organized. The two major groups are biotic components and abiotic components.

Abiotic components

These are the non-living physical and chemical components: sunlight, water, air, soil, minerals, temperature, pH, salinity and nutrients. They supply the conditions and raw materials required for life.

Biotic components

All living members collectively form the biotic community. Based on how they obtain food, they can be grouped into producers, consumers and decomposers.

1 · PRODUCERS

Autotrophs

Green plants, trees and algae prepare their own food mainly through photosynthesis. Chlorophyll captures light energy and helps convert carbon dioxide and water into carbohydrates.

6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
2 · CONSUMERS

Heterotrophs

Consumers cannot synthesize their own food. Primary consumers or herbivores feed directly on plants. Secondary consumers feed on primary consumers, while tertiary consumers occupy still higher feeding levels.

Examples: goat/deer/cow → herbivores; frog/cat/snake → secondary consumers; hawk/eagle → tertiary consumers.

3 · DECOMPOSERS

Nature's recyclers

Bacteria and fungi act on dead producers and consumers and convert complex organic matter into simpler substances. During decomposition, inorganic nutrients are released back to soil or water and can again be used by producers.

04 · FUNCTION OF AN ECOSYSTEM

Function means: what processes keep it working?

🌞 Energy capture

Producers capture solar energy and convert it into chemical energy stored in food.

🍽️ Energy transfer

Consumers obtain energy by feeding on producers or other consumers.

🍂 Decomposition

Dead matter is broken down by microorganisms.

♻️ Nutrient cycling

Minerals released during decomposition return to the abiotic environment and are reused.

Sun→Producer→Consumer→Dead matter→Decomposer→Nutrients→Producer again
Notice the difference: energy flows mainly in one direction, but matter and nutrients are recycled.
05 · ENERGY FLOW

Why does energy become less available at higher trophic levels?

Energy enters most ecosystems as sunlight. Producers convert a small part of this solar energy into chemical energy. When herbivores eat plants, only part of the stored energy becomes available to them. At every feeding level a large amount is used in respiration, movement, growth and maintenance and is ultimately lost as heat.

The source notes that around 80–90% of potential energy may be lost at each level. This is why energy decreases sharply as we move from producers to primary, secondary and tertiary consumers.

06 · FOOD CHAIN

A step-by-step pathway of eating and being eaten.

A food chain is a sequence through which food energy passes from plants through a series of organisms. It usually begins with plant life and proceeds through consumers. When organisms die, decomposers break their remains down and the released nutrients can again be used by plants.

Grazing food chain

Starts with living green plants and passes to herbivores and then carnivores.

Detritus food chain

Starts with dead organic matter. Detritus feeders and decomposers use this material, connecting dead matter back into the functioning ecosystem.

07 · FOOD WEB

Real ecosystems are networks, not single straight chains.

A food web is the interlocking pattern formed when many food chains are connected. Grass may be eaten by insects, rats and deer; these organisms may in turn be eaten by snakes, foxes, birds or larger predators.

Because organisms often have more than one food source and more than one predator, a food web gives a more realistic picture of feeding relationships than a single food chain.

08 · ECOLOGICAL SUCCESSION

An ecosystem can change gradually over time.

Ecological succession describes the gradual replacement of one biological community by another over time. A bare or disturbed area may first be occupied by hardy pioneer organisms. These organisms modify the environment, allowing grasses and herbs to establish, followed later by shrubs and trees where climate and soil permit.

09 · TERRESTRIAL ECOSYSTEMS

Forest, grassland and desert ecosystems.

🌳

Forest ecosystem

Abiotic: soil, atmosphere, water, minerals and characteristic litter are important components.

Producers: trees dominate, along with shrubs, climbers, epiphytes and ground vegetation. Examples in the source include teak, pine, cedar, ferns and lichens.

Consumers: herbivores feed on vegetation; carnivores and top predators occupy higher levels.

Decomposers: fungi and bacteria act on litter and dead organisms, returning nutrients to the soil.

🌾

Grassland ecosystem

Abiotic: water, carbon dioxide and mineral nutrients such as nitrates, phosphates and sulphates.

Producers: grasses, herbs and shrubs.

Primary consumers: insects, cattle, buffalo, deer and sheep.

Secondary consumers: lizards, birds, snakes, jackals and foxes. Hawks and eagles may act as tertiary consumers.

Decomposers: fungi and bacteria.

🏜️

Desert ecosystem

Deserts are characterized by low rainfall, high water stress and large temperature variations. Producers are adapted to conserve water; animals often avoid daytime heat or have physiological adaptations that reduce water loss.

10 · AQUATIC ECOSYSTEMS

Water chemistry and salinity strongly influence aquatic life.

Aquatic ecosystems are broadly divided into freshwater life zones such as ponds, streams, lakes and rivers, and saltwater life zones such as oceans and estuaries.

🏞️

Pond / Lake ecosystem

These standing-water ecosystems contain abiotic factors such as water, dissolved nutrients, light and temperature. Producers include phytoplankton and aquatic plants; consumers include zooplankton, insects, fishes and other animals; bacteria and fungi act as decomposers.

🏞️

River / Stream ecosystem

Abiotic: flowing water, light, temperature, chemistry and the stream-bed substrate.

Producers: phytoplankton, algae, water grasses and amphibious plants.

Primary consumers: water insects, snails and fishes feeding on producers. Birds may act as secondary consumers. Bacteria and fungi decompose dead matter.

🌊

Ocean ecosystem

Oceans are saline ecosystems with enormous spatial variation in light, temperature, pressure and depth. Phytoplankton form an important producer base supporting zooplankton, fishes and higher consumers.

🌊🏞️

Estuarine ecosystem

An estuary is a partially enclosed coastal area at the mouth of a river where freshwater mixes with seawater. Tidal action repeatedly changes water conditions.

Abiotic: temperature, pH, sodium and potassium salts and nutrients.

Producers: marsh grasses, seaweeds, sea-grasses and phytoplankton.

Consumers: oysters, crabs, seabirds and small fishes.

Decomposers: bacteria, fungi and actinomycetes.

Estuaries are nutrient-rich transition zones and are important food-producing environments, so protection from pollution is essential.
