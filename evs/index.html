01 · ECOLOGY AND ECOSYSTEM

From habitat to a complete interacting system

The word ecology was given by German biologist Haeckel in 1869. It comes from the Greek words Oikos, meaning house, habitat or place of living, and Logos, meaning study. Ecology studies interactions among organisms and between organisms and their environment.

The term ecosystem was proposed by A. G. Tansley in 1935. An ecosystem contains the biological community in a place together with the physical and chemical factors of its non-living environment. Energy and matter move between these components.

02 · STRUCTURE OF AN ECOSYSTEM

Biotic and abiotic components work together

The structure of an ecosystem refers to the components present in it. Biotic components are the living members—plants, animals and microorganisms. Abiotic components include air, water, soil, sunlight, temperature, minerals, nutrients, pH and other physical and chemical conditions.

BIOTIC

Living community

Producers, consumers and decomposers together form the living community. They interact through feeding, competition, predation and decomposition.

ABIOTIC

Non-living environment

Light, water, soil, air, climate and nutrients determine which organisms can live in a habitat and how productive that ecosystem can become.

Producers or autotrophs

Green plants and algae prepare their own food by photosynthesis. Chlorophyll captures sunlight and enables carbon dioxide and water to be converted into carbohydrates. Producers therefore form the energy base of most ecosystems.

6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

Consumers or heterotrophs

Primary consumers are herbivores that feed directly on plants. Secondary consumers feed on herbivores. Tertiary consumers feed at still higher levels. Omnivores eat both plants and animals, while detritivores consume dead organic material and wastes.

Decomposers

Bacteria and fungi break down dead producers and consumers into simpler substances. Inorganic nutrients are released and returned to soil or water, where producers can use them again.

03 · FUNCTIONS OF AN ECOSYSTEM

Food production, energy transfer and nutrient cycling

The primary function of an ecosystem is the production of food through photosynthesis. The secondary function is the transfer of this stored energy to consumers. The tertiary function is decomposition and nutrient recycling when organisms die.

04 · ENERGY FLOW

Why energy decreases at higher trophic levels

Solar energy enters the ecosystem through producers. Plants transform light energy into chemical energy. When an herbivore eats a plant, only part of this energy reaches the herbivore. At each later trophic level, organisms use energy for respiration, movement, growth and maintenance, so a large fraction is released as heat.

The material notes that roughly 80–90% of potential energy may be lost at each feeding level. This is why top consumers are usually fewer than organisms at lower levels.

06 · FOOD WEB

Real food webs contain real organisms and many feeding links

A food web is formed when several food chains interconnect. Grass may feed many herbivores; those herbivores may be eaten by different predators; smaller predators may themselves become prey. This makes the natural ecosystem a network rather than a single straight line.

07 · ECOLOGICAL PYRAMIDS

Use natural populations to understand the pyramid

An ecological pyramid compares trophic levels in terms of numbers, biomass or energy. Producers form the base because they support all higher levels. Energy decreases upward because energy is used and released as heat at each transfer.

Pyramid of Numbers

Shows the number of individual organisms at each trophic level.

Pyramid of Biomass

08 · FOREST ECOSYSTEM

A tree-dominated ecosystem with high biodiversity

Forests occur in areas receiving moderate to high rainfall and support many plants, animals and microorganisms. Producers include trees, shrubs, climbers, epiphytes and ground vegetation. Herbivores occupy primary consumer levels, carnivores occupy higher levels, and bacteria and fungi decompose litter and dead organisms.

09 · GRASSLAND ECOSYSTEM

Open ecosystems dominated by grasses

Grasslands contain grasses, herbs and some shrubs. Primary consumers include insects, cattle, buffaloes, deer and sheep; secondary consumers include lizards, birds, snakes, jackals and foxes; hawks and eagles may occupy higher levels.

10 · DESERT ECOSYSTEM

Life under severe water limitation

Deserts receive very little rainfall. Producers such as cacti and other succulents store water and reduce water loss. Many animals avoid daytime heat and become active at night. Decomposition is slower because vegetation and dead organic matter are limited.

11 · POND ECOSYSTEM

A standing freshwater ecosystem

Ponds are lentic or standing-water ecosystems. Producers include phytoplankton and aquatic plants. Zooplankton are important primary consumers, insects and small fishes form higher consumer levels, and fungi and bacteria recycle dead matter.

12 · LAKE ECOSYSTEM

A larger standing freshwater ecosystem

Lakes may be oligotrophic, eutrophic or dystrophic depending on nutrient conditions. The shallow near-shore region is the littoral zone, open sunlit water is the limnetic zone, deeper dark water is the profundal zone and the lake bottom forms the benthic zone.

13 · RIVER AND STREAM ECOSYSTEM

Flowing water changes oxygen and habitat conditions

Streams and rivers are free-flowing freshwater systems. Continuous mixing with air usually increases dissolved oxygen. Producers include algae, phytoplankton and aquatic plants; consumers include water insects, snails, fishes and birds.

14 · OCEAN / MARINE ECOSYSTEM

A vast saltwater ecosystem

Oceans contain saline water and support enormous biodiversity. The euphotic region receives enough light for photosynthesis, while deeper regions receive less or no light. Phytoplankton and seaweeds form the producer base, supporting crustaceans, molluscs, fish and higher consumers.

15 · ESTUARINE ECOSYSTEM

Where freshwater and seawater meet

An estuary is a partially enclosed coastal area at a river mouth where freshwater mixes with seawater. Tides continually change salinity and water conditions. Producers include marsh grasses, sea-grasses, seaweeds and phytoplankton, while consumers include oysters, crabs, fishes and seabirds.
