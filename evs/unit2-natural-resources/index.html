UNIT 2 · NATURAL RESOURCES

Natural Resources: use, pressure, degradation and conservation.

Natural resources are the goods and services supplied by nature that support life and human society. This unit studies renewable and non-renewable resources and then examines land, forest, water and energy resources in detail, including the environmental and social problems created by overuse.

01 · NATURAL RESOURCES

What makes something a natural resource?

Natural resources include living or biotic resources such as plants, animals and microorganisms, and non-living or abiotic resources such as air, water, soil, minerals, climate and solar energy. Human beings depend on these resources for food, water, shelter, energy, transport, industry and economic activity. They also support ecological processes that make life possible.

Renewable resources

A renewable resource can be replenished through natural cycles within a useful human time scale when its rate of use does not exceed its rate of renewal. Crops and forests can regenerate biologically; water is renewed through the hydrological cycle; and solar energy is treated as renewable because its supply is effectively inexhaustible on the human time scale.

Non-renewable resources

Non-renewable resources occur in finite stocks or are formed so slowly that their replacement is negligible on a human time scale. Fossil fuels, metallic ores and many mineral deposits belong to this group. Some non-renewable materials, especially metals, can be recovered and recycled; fuels such as coal and petroleum are consumed when used and cannot be recycled as fuels.

02 · LAND RESOURCES

Land use, land-cover change and land degradation

Land is a basic resource on which agriculture, forests, settlements, roads, industries and natural habitats depend. Land cover describes what physically covers the surface—forest, grass, crops, water, buildings or bare soil. Land use describes how people use that land—for farming, housing, industry, transport, conservation and other purposes. A change in land use often produces a change in land cover; for example, converting forest into agricultural fields removes natural vegetation and changes soil, runoff and habitat conditions.

Land degradation

Land degradation is a decline in the quality and productive capacity of land. It can involve loss of soil fertility and nutrients, reduction of vegetation cover, changes in soil structure, contamination, salinization and increased erosion. Human activities such as deforestation, overgrazing, mining, poorly managed irrigation, intensive cultivation and construction can accelerate degradation.

Soil erosion

Soil erosion is the removal of the upper layer of soil by agents such as flowing water and wind. The topsoil contains much of the organic matter and plant-available nutrients, so its loss reduces agricultural productivity. Vegetation protects soil by intercepting rainfall, binding particles with roots and reducing runoff. Removing vegetation therefore makes erosion more severe, particularly on slopes.

Vegetation loss→soil exposed→greater runoff/wind action→topsoil loss→lower fertility

Desertification

Desertification is land degradation in dry regions in which productive land progressively loses vegetation and soil quality and becomes increasingly desert-like. Repeated drought can contribute, but human pressures such as overgrazing, excessive cultivation, deforestation and poor water management can intensify the process. Conservation therefore requires maintaining vegetation, controlling erosion, using water carefully and matching land use to the land's ecological capacity.

03 · FOREST RESOURCES

Types, scope, deforestation and development pressures

A forest is a biological community dominated by trees and other woody vegetation. Forests have both direct economic value and ecological value. They supply timber, fuelwood, fibres, gums, resins, medicines and other products. At the same time they provide habitat, protect soil, influence the water cycle, moderate local climate, store carbon and support biodiversity.

Productive / economic role

Timber and fuel, pulp and paper raw material, fibres, medicines, gums, resins, food and other forest products support livelihoods and industries.

Ecological role

Forests provide wildlife habitat, reduce erosion, regulate runoff, recycle nutrients, influence rainfall and climate, absorb carbon dioxide and help moderate pollution.

Deforestation

Deforestation is the removal or destruction of forest cover. The study material identifies development projects, mining, industrial demand for wood, fuel requirements, shifting cultivation, forest fires and overgrazing among important pressures. When forest is cleared, the effect is not limited to the trees: habitat is fragmented, biodiversity declines, soil becomes exposed, runoff can increase, erosion accelerates and local communities may lose access to resources on which they depend.

Mining and forests

Mining extracts mineral resources and fossil fuels from the earth. Surface mining works shallow deposits, while underground mining reaches deeper deposits. Mining can remove vegetation and soil, fragment habitat, create pits and waste material, generate dust and noise, contaminate soil and water, and increase slope instability. Thus a mineral resource may provide economic benefit while its extraction imposes environmental costs on the surrounding forest landscape.

Dams and forests

Large reservoirs can submerge forest and farmland and transform a river ecosystem. Dams can provide irrigation water, domestic and industrial water, hydropower and flood control, but their environmental costs may include habitat loss, changes to aquatic ecosystems, siltation, waterlogging and changes in downstream flow. Therefore their benefits must be evaluated together with ecological and social impacts.

Impact on tribal and forest-dependent populations

For many tribal and indigenous communities, forests are not simply a source of timber. They may provide food, fuel, medicines, grazing, employment, cultural identity and traditional living space. Mining, reservoirs and other large projects can therefore cause displacement and loss of livelihood as well as cultural and social disruption. Rehabilitation is not only a question of replacing a house; it also involves restoring access to livelihood, community networks and resources.

04 · WATER RESOURCES

Surface water, groundwater and the consequences of over-exploitation

Fresh water is essential for life and is required for agriculture, households, industry and ecosystem functioning. Surface water occurs in rivers, lakes, ponds and reservoirs. Groundwater is stored below the surface in soil and rock formations and is accessed through wells and borewells. Both sources are linked through the hydrological cycle.

Over-exploitation of groundwater

Groundwater remains sustainable only when withdrawal is reasonably balanced by recharge. If pumping repeatedly exceeds recharge, the water table falls. Wells may dry up or require deeper pumping. In some aquifers, removal of water allows sediments to compact, causing land subsidence. Urban construction can worsen the problem by covering permeable ground and reducing the infiltration of rainwater.

Floods

A flood occurs when water exceeds the carrying capacity of its normal channel and spreads over surrounding land. Heavy rainfall, rapid snowmelt and sudden releases can trigger flooding. Deforestation, mining, overgrazing and poorly planned development may increase surface runoff or reduce the capacity of natural drainage systems. Flood management can combine forecasting and warning, appropriate reservoir and channel management, protection of floodplains and catchment vegetation, and measures that improve infiltration.

Drought

Drought is a prolonged condition of water scarcity associated with inadequate rainfall and/or demand exceeding available water. Its effects include drinking-water shortage, crop failure, livestock stress, food insecurity, deterioration of water quality and pressure on natural resources. Rainwater harvesting, groundwater recharge, efficient irrigation such as drip irrigation, drought-adapted cropping and afforestation can improve resilience.

Conflicts over water

Rivers and aquifers do not necessarily follow political boundaries. When several regions depend on the same water source, unequal distribution, variable rainfall and growing demand can create disputes. The source material cites the Cauvery sharing issue between Karnataka and Tamil Nadu and the Indus between India and Pakistan as examples of interstate and international dimensions of water sharing.

Water-resource principle: management must consider the entire basin—rainfall, catchment vegetation, surface flow, groundwater recharge, agriculture, cities, industries and ecosystems are interconnected.
05 · ENERGY RESOURCES

Growing energy needs and the shift toward alternate sources

Energy is required for households, transport, agriculture, industry, communication and almost every modern economic activity. As population, urbanization and industrial activity grow, total energy demand rises. The environmental question is therefore not simply how to produce more energy, but how to meet useful energy needs efficiently while reducing depletion and pollution.

Renewable energy sources

Solar

Solar radiation can be converted directly to electricity using photovoltaic cells or used as heat in devices such as solar water heaters.

Wind

Wind turbines convert the kinetic energy of moving air into rotational mechanical energy and then electricity. Groups of turbines form wind farms.

Ocean / tidal

Tidal systems use the periodic movement of seawater caused mainly by gravitational interactions of the Earth, Moon and Sun.

Geothermal

Geothermal energy uses heat from within the Earth for heating or electricity generation where suitable geological conditions exist.

Biomass / biogas

Organic matter from plants, animals and wastes can provide energy. Anaerobic digestion can produce biogas rich in methane.

Hydropower

Flowing or falling water can drive turbines. It is renewable, but large reservoirs can have substantial ecological and social impacts.

Non-renewable energy sources

Coal is a solid fossil fuel and its combustion releases carbon dioxide and other pollutants. Petroleum is a liquid mixture of hydrocarbons refined into fuels and other products. Natural gas consists mainly of methane and is another fossil fuel. Nuclear energy does not depend on fossil-fuel combustion, but uranium fuel is finite and nuclear power requires careful management of radiation risks and radioactive wastes.

Why alternate energy sources matter

Greater use of renewable sources can reduce dependence on finite fossil fuels and, during operation, many renewable technologies produce much lower air pollution and greenhouse-gas emissions. However, every energy technology uses land, materials and infrastructure. Sustainable energy planning therefore combines renewable generation with energy conservation, efficiency, appropriate siting, storage and responsible management of environmental impacts.
