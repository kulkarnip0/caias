01 · ECOLOGY AND ECOSYSTEM

From “where an organism lives” to “how the whole system works”

The word ecology was given by German biologist Haeckel in 1869. It comes from the Greek words Oikos, meaning house, habitat or place of living, and Logos, meaning study. Danish botanist Eugenius Warming later elaborated the idea of ecology.

Ecology studies interactions among organisms and between organisms and their environment. The environment has a living part—plants, animals and microorganisms—and a non-living part that includes light, water, wind, nutrients in soil, heat, solar radiation and the atmosphere. An organism therefore cannot be understood in isolation: its survival depends on conditions around it and on other organisms.

The term ecosystem was proposed by A. G. Tansley in 1935. An ecosystem contains the biological community occurring in a place together with the physical and chemical factors of its non-living environment. A pond, desert, forest, estuary and ocean are examples. The ecosystem is called the basic functional unit of ecology because it shows organisms interacting with one another and with the environment while exchanging energy and matter.

Simple way to remember: Ecology is the study. The ecosystem is the working unit being studied. For example, ecology asks how deer, plants, soil, rainfall and predators interact inside a forest ecosystem.
02 · STRUCTURE OF AN ECOSYSTEM

What is an ecosystem made of?

The word structure refers to the different components present in an ecosystem. These components are divided into two broad groups: biotic components, which are living, and abiotic components, which are non-living.

BIOTIC COMPONENTS

The living community

Plants, animals and microorganisms collectively form the biotic community. Based on how they obtain food, these living members are grouped into producers, consumers and decomposers.

ABIOTIC COMPONENTS

The non-living environment

Climate, soil, water, air and energy form the physical environment. Physical components include air, water, soil, sunlight, climate, nutrients and living space. Chemical components supply essential nutrients and include organic substances such as proteins, lipids and carbohydrates and inorganic mineral elements.

1. Producers or autotrophs

Producers prepare their own food. Green plants and trees contain chlorophyll, which helps convert carbon dioxide and water into carbohydrates in the presence of sunlight. This process is photosynthesis. Producers are therefore the entry point through which solar energy becomes chemical energy available to the biological community.

6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂   (in the presence of sunlight and chlorophyll)

2. Consumers or heterotrophs

Consumers cannot synthesize their own food and depend on other organisms. Primary consumers, also called herbivores or plant eaters, directly depend on plants; examples include insects, rats, goats, deer and cows. Secondary consumers, or primary carnivores, feed on herbivores; examples include frogs, cats, snakes and small birds. Tertiary consumers feed on secondary consumers and include organisms such as hawks, eagles, tigers and lions.

The material also identifies omnivores, which feed on both plants and animals, such as humans, rats and birds, and detritivores, which feed on dead organisms, wastes and partially decomposed matter, such as termites, earthworms and ants.

3. Decomposers

Decomposers such as bacteria and fungi attack the dead bodies of producers and consumers and break them into simpler compounds. During decomposition, inorganic nutrients are released. These nutrients become available in the environment and can again be used by producers to synthesize food. Decomposition therefore connects death back to new biological production.

03 · TYPES OF ECOSYSTEM

Natural ecosystems and artificial ecosystems

Natural ecosystems operate under natural conditions. Terrestrial ecosystems are related to land and include forests, grasslands and deserts. Aquatic ecosystems are related to water. Freshwater ecosystems include running-water systems such as rivers and streams and standing-water systems such as ponds and lakes. Marine ecosystems include seas and seashores.

Artificial or man-made ecosystems are operated or maintained by human beings. Croplands and gardens are examples. Their components may still include producers, consumers, decomposers and abiotic factors, but human management plays an important role in maintaining the system.

04 · FUNCTIONS OF AN ECOSYSTEM

Energy must flow and nutrients must cycle

The major function of an ecosystem is to permit the flow of energy and the cycling of nutrients. The source groups ecosystem function into three levels. The primary function is the manufacture of food material through photosynthesis. The secondary function is the distribution of energy in the form of food to consumers. The tertiary function begins when organisms die: decomposers break down dead matter and initiate nutrient cycling.

To understand ecosystem functioning in detail, we therefore study energy and material flow, food chains, food webs and ecological pyramids. These topics are not separate ideas; they describe different views of the same functioning ecosystem.

05 · ENERGY FLOW IN ECOSYSTEMS

Sunlight becomes food energy, then moves through feeding levels

Energy is essential for all living organisms. Solar energy is the main source of energy entering the ecosystem. Primary producers transform solar energy into chemical energy during photosynthesis. Plants use some of this chemical energy for their own growth and maintenance, while the remaining energy can pass to consumers when plants are eaten.

Energy therefore enters through photosynthesis and passes through different trophic or feeding levels. The first law of thermodynamics states that energy cannot be created or destroyed but can be transformed from one form to another. In an ecosystem, sunlight is converted into chemical energy and consumers ultimately transform much of that energy into heat.

The second law explains why less useful energy remains at higher levels: whenever energy is transformed, some is released as heat. Respiration, movement, work, running and hunting all use energy. The PDF notes that roughly 80–90% of potential energy may be lost as heat at each feeding level.

T1 · Producers · greatest energy base
T2 · Primary consumers / herbivores
T3 · Secondary consumers / carnivores
T4 · Tertiary consumers · least available energy
Key distinction: Energy moves through an ecosystem and is progressively dissipated as heat. Nutrients, however, can move from the abiotic environment into organisms and return again through decomposition.
06 · FOOD CHAINS AND TROPHIC LEVELS

A sequence of eating and being eaten

A food chain is the transfer of food energy from plants through a series of organisms. It normally starts with plant life and proceeds through consumers. When organisms die, microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi decompose them and release nutrients that can again be used by plants.

The different steps through which food energy passes are called trophic levels. Producers occupy T1, herbivores or primary consumers T2, secondary consumers T3, and tertiary consumers T4. The material also places decomposers at the final level because they act on dead organic matter.

Grazing food chain

This begins with living green plants. Energy moves from producers to herbivores and then to carnivores. The material notes grazing food chains in grassland and pond ecosystems.

Detritus food chain

This begins with dead organic matter from plants and animals and proceeds through organisms that use this material. Grazing and detritus pathways are interconnected rather than isolated.

07 · FOOD WEB

Many food chains interlock to form a network

A food web is the interlocking pattern of many food chains. An organism is rarely connected to only one food source or one predator. Grass, for example, may be eaten by insects, rats and deer, which may then be eaten by snakes, foxes, tigers or other carnivores.

A linear food chain is more vulnerable: if one species is severely affected, organisms at subsequent trophic levels may also be affected. In a food web there are more feeding alternatives, so disturbance of one link may not affect every higher level as seriously.

Food chains and food webs are important because energy flow and nutrient cycling occur through them, they help regulate population sizes and ecological balance, and they provide pathways through which non-biodegradable substances may move. When the concentration of such substances increases at successive trophic levels, the process is called biomagnification.

08 · ECOLOGICAL PYRAMIDS

A graphical way to compare trophic levels

An ecological pyramid graphically represents the structure and function of trophic levels, with producers forming the base and successive consumer levels forming the upper portions. Because energy is lost at each level, the energy available generally becomes progressively smaller toward the top.

Pyramid of Numbers

Represents the number of individual organisms at each trophic level. In a grassland example, grasses are numerous, rats are fewer, snakes fewer still and eagles occupy the upper level.

Pyramid of Biomass

Represents the total mass or weight of biological material at each trophic level. In a forest, trees contribute a large producer biomass, while herbivores, carnivores and top consumers progressively contribute less biomass.

Pyramid of Energy

Shows energy flow at each trophic level and the role organisms play in energy transfer. It emphasizes the large loss of energy as we move upward.

09 · FOREST ECOSYSTEM

A tree-dominated ecosystem supporting high biological diversity

A forest is an area with a high density of trees. Forest ecosystems occur in areas receiving moderate to high rainfall and support many animals and birds. The PDF classifies forests according to climate into tropical rain forests, tropical deciduous forests, tropical scrub forests, temperate rain forests and temperate deciduous forests.

Forest ecosystems are associated with adequate rainfall, organic-rich soil and abundant biological activity. Forests help maintain climate and rainfall and support wildlife and biodiversity. Their structure includes inorganic and organic compounds in the soil and atmosphere and characteristic forest litter.

Producers are mainly trees, together with climbers, epiphytes, shrubs and ground vegetation. Examples listed include Dalbergia, teak (Tectona grandis), lichens, ferns, pine and cedar. Primary consumers include insects, mice, deer, squirrels and larger herbivores such as elephants and giraffes. Secondary consumers include lizards, snakes, birds and foxes; tigers and lions are listed as tertiary consumers. Bacteria and fungi act as decomposers.

Forest ecosystems provide many environmental services: nutrient cycling, biodiversity maintenance, wildlife habitat, influence on rainfall, regulation of stream flow, water storage, reduction of flooding, prevention of soil erosion and reclamation of degraded land. They also supply traditional resources such as firewood, timber, fruits, gums, herbs and drugs.

10 · GRASSLAND ECOSYSTEM

Open ecosystems dominated by grasses

Grasslands occupy large open areas dominated by grasses, although some trees and shrubs may occur. Limited grazing can improve net primary production, while overgrazing can degrade the ecosystem and contribute to desertification.

The material identifies tropical, temperate and polar grasslands. Tropical grasslands or savannas occur near tropical rain forests, have high temperatures and moderate rainfall, and contain tall grasses with scattered shrubs and stunted trees. Temperate grasslands experience cold winters and hot summers; grazing and summer fires limit shrubs and trees. Polar grasslands occur in Arctic regions with severe cold, strong winds, ice and snow.

Grassland soils are described as rich in nutrients and organic matter. Producers include grasses, herbs and shrubs. Primary consumers include insects, cows, buffaloes, deer and sheep. Lizards, birds, snakes, jackals and foxes are secondary consumers, while hawks and eagles are tertiary consumers. Fungi and bacteria decompose dead organic matter.

Grasslands are important for livestock, milk and dairy production, grazing, biodiversity and habitat for many plants, birds and other animals.

11 · DESERT ECOSYSTEM

Life under severe water limitation

Deserts are characterized by very low rainfall—less than about 25 cm annually in the source—and dry atmospheric conditions. They are grouped into tropical, temperate and cold deserts. The Sahara and Thar are given as tropical examples, Atacama and Mojave as temperate examples, and Gobi and Ladakh as cold-desert examples.

Important abiotic conditions include intense solar radiation, strong winds, scarcity of moisture, poor soil organic matter and low nutrient cycling. Vegetation is therefore sparse.

Desert producers include succulents such as cacti, shrubs, bushes, grasses and a few trees. Succulents store water and may possess waxy coatings and thorns. Many desert consumers live in holes and become active at night; some obtain water from the seeds they eat. Examples include locusts, scorpions, snakes and camels. Because dead organic matter is limited, decomposer activity is also relatively low, with fungi and bacteria performing decomposition.

12 · POND ECOSYSTEM

A standing freshwater ecosystem

A pond is a freshwater aquatic ecosystem with stagnant water. It receives water during the rainy season and supports algae, aquatic plants, insects, fishes and birds. Ponds are described as lentic ecosystems, meaning standing-water systems, and may be seasonal. Their limited water volume can also make them vulnerable to pollution.

Abiotic components include temperature, light, water and organic and inorganic compounds. Producers include photosynthetic organisms such as phytoplankton and aquatic plants. The material lists Nostoc, Anabaena and Cosmarium among floating microscopic forms and Hydrilla and other floating/submerged plants.

Primary consumers include zooplankton that feed on phytoplankton. Secondary consumers include water beetles and small fishes, while large fishes may act as tertiary consumers. Fungi, bacteria and flagellates decompose dead matter and release nutrients for reuse by green plants.

Ponds are important habitats for fish, birds, plants, crustaceans and insects such as dragonflies and pond skaters. They are widespread, may provide water to animals and people, and add ecological and aesthetic value.

13 · LAKE ECOSYSTEM

A larger standing freshwater system with distinct zones

Lakes receive water from rainfall, melting snow and streams. The source distinguishes oligotrophic lakes with low nutrient concentrations, eutrophic lakes enriched with nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus, dystrophic lakes with low pH and humic brown water, and volcanic lakes.

According to depth and distance from shore, a lake can be divided into four zones. The littoral zone is shallow near-shore water. The limnetic zone is open water where sunlight penetrates effectively. The profundal zone is deeper and darker, while the benthic zone lies at the bottom.

Abiotic components include temperature, light, oxygen, carbon dioxide and organic substances. Producers include phytoplankton, algae and submerged, floating or amphibious green plants. Zooplankton are primary consumers; insects and small fishes act as secondary consumers; large fishes are tertiary consumers. Bacteria, fungi and actinomycetes act as decomposers.

14 · RIVER AND STREAM ECOSYSTEM

Flowing freshwater changes the ecological conditions

Streams and rivers are free-flowing freshwater systems. Moving water mixes with air and is usually well oxygenated. The material notes higher dissolved oxygen due to mixing and the deposition of nutrients by rivers.

Abiotic components include flowing water, light, temperature, water chemistry and the bottom substrate. Producers include phytoplankton, algae, water grasses and amphibious aquatic plants. Primary consumers include water insects, snails and fishes that feed on producers. Birds are listed as secondary consumers, while bacteria and fungi decompose dead plants and animals.

15 · OCEAN / MARINE ECOSYSTEM

The planet's vast saltwater ecosystem

Oceans cover more than two-thirds of Earth's surface and are characterized by high concentrations of salts and minerals. The source divides ocean life into a relatively warm, nutrient-rich shallow coastal zone with high productivity and the deeper open sea.

The open sea is further divided vertically. The euphotic zone receives abundant light and supports high photosynthetic activity. The bathyal zone receives dim light. The abyssal zone is very deep and dark.

Abiotic factors include temperature, light, salinity and mineral salts such as sodium chloride, potassium, calcium and magnesium compounds. Producers include phytoplankton such as diatoms and unicellular algae as well as seaweeds and other marine plants. Primary consumers include crustaceans, molluscs and fish; herring and mackerel are listed among secondary consumers, and cod and haddock among higher consumers. Bacteria and some fungi decompose dead organic matter.

Marine ecosystems are rich in biodiversity, support commercial activities and help moderate temperature.

16 · ESTUARINE ECOSYSTEM

Where freshwater and seawater meet

An estuary is a partially enclosed coastal area at the mouth of a river where seawater mixes with freshwater. It is strongly affected by tides and is generally rich in nutrients. Because river flow and tides change, water characteristics also change periodically, so organisms living here require a wide tolerance to environmental variation.

The source notes seasonal changes in salinity, with salinity highest during summer and lowest during winter. Abiotic components include temperature, pH, sodium and potassium salts and nutrients. Producers include marsh grasses, seaweeds, sea-grasses and phytoplankton. Consumers include oysters, crabs, seabirds and small fishes. Bacteria, fungi and actinomycetes function as decomposers.

Estuaries have high food potential and are valuable to people. Because of their ecological productivity and transitional nature, the material emphasizes protecting estuaries from pollution.
