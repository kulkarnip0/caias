What is environmental pollution?

WHAT? Start with a simple idea

Imagine a clean glass of water. If someone adds dirty oil, the water is no longer safe. Pollution means adding harmful substances or energy to our surroundings faster than nature can safely handle them.

WHY? What causes it or makes it important?

Pollutants can be gases, particles, chemicals, waste, harmful microorganisms, excess heat, noise or radiation. A pollutant may enter air, water or soil, travel through the environment and affect living organisms.

WHAT HAPPENS? Understand the effects

Think about a busy road: vehicle exhaust enters the air; litter can wash into drains; loud horns disturb people. One place can have several types of pollution at once.

HOW CAN WE HELP?

Preventing pollution at its source is usually better than trying to clean it up afterward.

Think and explain: Can you give one example of this topic from your home, school, street or city? Explain the cause, one possible effect and one practical solution.

DEEPER EXPLANATION · Learn step by step

1. A pollutant is something that causes pollution. It may be a substance, such as smoke, plastic or a chemical, or a form of energy, such as unwanted noise or excess heat. A substance is not automatically harmful in every quantity: the amount, place, duration and sensitivity of exposed organisms matter.

2. Pollution can be understood as a journey. First, there is a source: a vehicle, factory, household or farm. Next, a pollutant enters air, water or land. Then it moves through wind, rain, runoff or food chains. Finally, people, animals or plants may be exposed. To solve a problem, we should identify every stage rather than only cleaning up visible dirt.

3. A useful distinction is between point sources and diffuse sources. A pipe releasing wastewater at one location is a point source. Fertilizer washed from many fields is a diffuse or non-point source. Diffuse pollution is harder to control because it comes from many scattered places.

4. Pollution control follows a simple order: avoid creating pollution; reduce it at the source; capture or treat what remains; dispose of residues safely; and monitor the environment. For a school example, reusable bottles prevent plastic waste, waste segregation improves collection, and regular cleaning prevents litter from reaching drains.

Air pollution — when the air becomes unhealthy

WHAT? Start with a simple idea

Air is an invisible mixture of gases. We breathe it every moment. Air pollution happens when smoke, tiny particles or harmful gases enter it in amounts that can harm health or nature.

WHY? What causes it or makes it important?

Vehicles, burning coal and waste, industries, construction dust and some household fuels release pollutants. Primary pollutants are released directly; secondary pollutants form when other substances react in air.

WHAT HAPPENS? Understand the effects

Tiny particles can enter our lungs. Polluted air can worsen asthma and other breathing and heart problems. Some gases damage plants, reduce visibility and help form smog.

HOW CAN WE HELP?

Use cleaner transport and fuels, control industrial emissions, avoid open waste burning, reduce dust and follow air-quality warnings.

Think and explain: Can you give one example of this topic from your home, school, street or city? Explain the cause, one possible effect and one practical solution.

DEEPER EXPLANATION · Learn step by step

1. The air around us contains mostly nitrogen and oxygen, along with smaller amounts of other gases. We need oxygen for respiration. A child can understand air pollution by imagining clean air as a shared classroom: if smoke or dust enters, everyone has to breathe it even if only one person caused it.

2. Particulate matter means tiny solid or liquid particles floating in air. PM10 refers to particles with aerodynamic diameter of 10 micrometres or less, and PM2.5 to 2.5 micrometres or less. Fine particles can travel deep into the respiratory system. Common gaseous pollutants include nitrogen dioxide, sulfur dioxide, carbon monoxide and ground-level ozone.

3. Ground-level ozone is different from the protective ozone layer high in the atmosphere. Near the ground, ozone can form when other pollutants react in sunlight and can irritate the respiratory system. Smog is a polluted haze that may involve particles and gases, often becoming worse under weather conditions that trap polluted air.

4. Air quality depends on both emissions and weather. Wind may disperse pollutants; stagnant air can let them build up. A practical classroom activity is to compare a busy road, a quiet garden and a construction site. Ask which sources of dust or exhaust are present and how exposure could be reduced.

5. Control measures include maintaining vehicles, cleaner public transport, avoiding open burning, industrial filters and scrubbers where appropriate, dust suppression at construction sites, and reducing dependence on highly polluting fuels. Trees help ecosystems but cannot replace controlling emissions at their sources.

03 · EVS UNIT III

Water pollution — when rivers and lakes become dirty

WHAT? Start with a simple idea

A river is like a moving water supply for plants, fish, animals and people. If sewage, chemicals or litter enter it, the water may become polluted.

WHY? What causes it or makes it important?

Untreated sewage, industrial wastewater, farm runoff carrying fertilizers or pesticides, oil and plastic waste are common causes. Rain can wash pollutants from streets into streams.

WHAT HAPPENS? Understand the effects

Contaminated water can spread disease. Too many nutrients can cause algal blooms; when algae die and decompose, dissolved oxygen can fall and fish may die.

HOW CAN WE HELP?

Treat sewage and industrial wastewater, reduce harmful runoff, protect wetlands and never throw waste into water bodies.

Think and explain: Can you give one example of this topic from your home, school, street or city? Explain the cause, one possible effect and one practical solution.

DEEPER EXPLANATION · Learn step by step

1. Water pollution may not always be visible. Clear-looking water can still contain microorganisms or dissolved chemicals. Similarly, muddy water is not automatically poisonous. Water safety requires appropriate testing and treatment, not judging only by colour or smell.

2. Domestic sewage contains wastewater from toilets, kitchens and washing. When untreated sewage enters a river, disease-causing microorganisms and organic matter may enter the water. Microorganisms decomposing organic matter consume dissolved oxygen. This is why wastewater treatment is important before discharge.

3. Fertilizers contain nutrients that help crops grow. If too much nitrogen or phosphorus reaches a lake, algae may grow rapidly. This is called nutrient enrichment or eutrophication. When large amounts of algae die, decomposition can lower dissolved oxygen, stressing or killing fish. This is a chain reaction: runoff → nutrients → algal growth → decomposition → oxygen shortage.

4. A wastewater treatment plant commonly uses several stages: screening removes large objects; settling separates some suspended solids; biological treatment uses microorganisms to break down organic matter; additional treatment may remove nutrients and pathogens. Treatment methods depend on the wastewater and the required discharge quality.

5. At home and school, do not pour oils, paints, medicines or chemicals into drains. Fix leaks, use water carefully, keep drains free of rubbish and support sanitation. Remember that conserving water and preventing water pollution are related but different actions.

04 · EVS UNIT III

Soil pollution — when the ground is contaminated

WHAT? Start with a simple idea

Soil is not just dust. It contains minerals, air, water and many tiny living organisms. Healthy soil supports plant roots and helps grow food.

WHY? What causes it or makes it important?

Improper disposal of chemicals, industrial waste, leaking fuel, excessive pesticide use and poorly managed dumps can contaminate soil.

WHAT HAPPENS? Understand the effects

Some contaminants harm soil organisms, reduce fertility, reach groundwater or enter crops. Contaminated land can be difficult and expensive to restore.

HOW CAN WE HELP?

Dispose of hazardous waste safely, prevent leaks, use chemicals responsibly, compost suitable organic matter and test suspected contaminated land.

Think and explain: Can you give one example of this topic from your home, school, street or city? Explain the cause, one possible effect and one practical solution.

DEEPER EXPLANATION · Learn step by step

1. Soil forms very slowly through weathering of rocks and the action of living organisms. It has spaces containing air and water, and it is home to earthworms, fungi, bacteria and other organisms. These living parts help recycle nutrients and support plant growth.

2. Soil contamination occurs when unwanted substances accumulate at harmful levels. Examples include heavy metals from some industrial activities, petroleum leaks and residues of certain chemicals. A pollutant may stay near the surface, attach to soil particles, move into groundwater or be taken up by plants depending on its properties.

3. Soil pollution and soil erosion are different. Pollution adds harmful contaminants; erosion removes soil, often through wind or water. Both can reduce land productivity, and the same land can suffer from both problems.

4. Imagine a school garden where used batteries are thrown into a planting bed. Some batteries contain materials that require special disposal. The right response is not simply to cover them with soil but to collect them through an appropriate waste system and avoid contamination.

5. Protection involves safe storage of fuels and chemicals, avoiding excessive use of pesticides, managing industrial waste, segregating batteries and electronics, composting uncontaminated organic matter, and restoring contaminated land using methods suited to the specific pollutant.

05 · EVS UNIT III

Noise pollution — when sound becomes harmful

WHAT? Start with a simple idea

Sound helps us speak and hear music. Noise pollution means unwanted or excessive sound that disturbs daily life or health.

WHY? What causes it or makes it important?

Traffic horns, aircraft, construction machinery, factories and very loud speakers can expose people to unwanted sound. Both loudness and how long exposure lasts matter.

WHAT HAPPENS? Understand the effects

Noise may interrupt sleep, make concentration difficult, increase stress and, at high levels, damage hearing.

HOW CAN WE HELP?

Reduce unnecessary honking, maintain machines, use quieter equipment, create noise barriers and protect hearing where needed.

Think and explain: Can you give one example of this topic from your home, school, street or city? Explain the cause, one possible effect and one practical solution.

DEEPER EXPLANATION · Learn step by step

1. Sound travels as vibrations through air and other materials. We measure sound levels using decibels, abbreviated dB. The decibel scale is logarithmic, so an increase in the number does not represent a simple linear increase in sound energy.

2. Noise becomes an environmental issue when unwanted sound disturbs people or wildlife. A short sound and a continuous sound of the same level may have different effects because exposure duration matters. Night-time noise can be especially disruptive when people are trying to sleep.

3. Common sources include road traffic, horns, trains, aircraft, construction, generators, industrial equipment and amplified music. Students can make a sound map of their school by noting locations that are usually quiet and locations where conversation becomes difficult.

4. The effects can include annoyance, disturbed sleep, difficulty concentrating and stress. Repeated exposure to sufficiently high sound levels can harm hearing. Wildlife may also change behaviour when human noise interferes with communication or movement.

5. Three basic control methods are to reduce noise at the source, interrupt its path and protect the receiver. Examples are quieter machinery, acoustic barriers and appropriate hearing protection in noisy workplaces. Community rules and thoughtful behaviour also matter.

06 · EVS UNIT III

Nuclear hazards and human health

WHAT? Start with a simple idea

Radiation is energy moving as waves or particles. Some radiation is ionizing, meaning it can damage cells. Nuclear hazards involve unsafe exposure to radioactive materials or ionizing radiation.

WHY? What causes it or makes it important?

Hazards may arise from accidents, unsafe handling or disposal of radioactive materials, or poor safety procedures. Risk depends on the radiation type, dose, exposure duration and pathway.

WHAT HAPPENS? Understand the effects

High doses can cause serious injury. Some exposures can increase long-term cancer risk. Safe medical and industrial uses are possible when radiation is carefully controlled.

HOW CAN WE HELP?

Use shielding, minimize unnecessary exposure time, maximize distance where appropriate, monitor radiation and follow strict storage and disposal rules.

Think and explain: Can you give one example of this topic from your home, school, street or city? Explain the cause, one possible effect and one practical solution.

DEEPER EXPLANATION · Learn step by step

1. Atoms are tiny building blocks of matter. Some atomic nuclei are unstable and release radiation as they change. This is radioactivity. Not all radiation is the same: visible light and radio waves are non-ionizing, while certain higher-energy radiation can ionize matter.

2. Ionizing radiation can be useful. Hospitals use it in carefully controlled imaging and cancer treatment, and industries may use it for inspection. The same property that makes it useful can also damage living cells if exposure is not properly controlled.

3. It is important to distinguish exposure from contamination. Exposure means radiation reaches a person. Contamination means radioactive material is present on or inside a person or in an environment where it should not be. The precautions can differ.

4. The basic protective principles are time, distance and shielding: reduce unnecessary time near a source, increase distance when possible, and use appropriate shielding. Specialized workers also use monitoring instruments and regulated procedures.

5. Nuclear waste requires classification, secure containment, monitoring and disposal systems designed for the type and duration of hazard. In an emergency, people should follow official instructions rather than guessing whether an area is safe.

07 · EVS UNIT III

Solid waste — what happens after we throw something away?

WHAT? Start with a simple idea

Solid waste is discarded material such as food scraps, paper, packaging, broken objects and industrial residues. Throwing something into a bin does not make it disappear.

WHY? What causes it or makes it important?

Cities generate household and commercial waste. Industries generate process residues that may be harmless, recyclable or hazardous. Mixing waste makes safe treatment more difficult.

WHAT HAPPENS? Understand the effects

Open dumping can attract pests, block drains and contaminate land or water. Burning mixed waste can release harmful smoke. Poorly managed hazardous waste creates additional risks.

HOW CAN WE HELP?

First reduce waste; then reuse, repair and recycle where possible. Segregate wet, dry and hazardous waste. Compost suitable organic material and send remaining waste to appropriate treatment or disposal.

Think and explain: Can you give one example of this topic from your home, school, street or city? Explain the cause, one possible effect and one practical solution.

DEEPER EXPLANATION · Learn step by step

1. A household bin often contains very different materials: banana peels, paper, plastic wrappers, glass, metal, used batteries and sanitary waste. Mixing them makes recovery and safe disposal difficult. The first lesson in waste management is to identify the type of waste before deciding what to do with it.

2. Biodegradable organic waste such as vegetable peels can be composted under suitable conditions. Dry recyclable materials may include clean paper, cardboard, some plastics, metal and glass, depending on local recycling facilities. Hazardous household items, including certain batteries, paints and chemicals, need separate handling.

3. The waste hierarchy begins with refusing unnecessary items and reducing consumption. Reuse means using an item again without turning it into raw material. Recycling processes discarded material into something usable. Disposal should be the last option when recovery is unsuitable.

4. Municipal waste management includes collection, segregation, transportation, material recovery, biological treatment of suitable organics, treatment of residual waste and sanitary landfilling of appropriate residues. Poorly designed open dumps can generate contaminated liquid called leachate and may release landfill gases.

5. Industrial waste is not one single category. A factory may generate reusable scrap, ordinary packaging, wastewater-treatment sludge or hazardous residues. The correct treatment depends on its properties. A waste audit can identify what is generated, how much, and where reduction is possible.

6. Case study for discussion: A neighbourhood begins separating wet kitchen waste from dry packaging. Wet waste goes to a properly managed composting facility, while clean recyclable materials go to sorting. Students can explain why contamination decreases and why public participation is necessary.

Climate change and global warming

WHAT? Start with a simple idea

Weather tells us what the sky is like today. Climate describes usual weather patterns over many years. Global warming means a long-term rise in Earth's average surface temperature.

WHY? What causes it or makes it important?

The natural greenhouse effect helps Earth stay warm. Burning fossil fuels and clearing forests increase greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide, strengthening this warming effect.

WHAT HAPPENS? Understand the effects

Climate change affects heat, rainfall, drought risk, heavy rainfall, sea levels, ecosystems and farming. The impacts differ by place and community.

HOW CAN WE HELP?

Cut greenhouse gas emissions through clean energy, efficient buildings and transport, and forest protection; also prepare for heat, flooding and water shortages.

Think and explain: Can you give one example of this topic from your home, school, street or city? Explain the cause, one possible effect and one practical solution.

DEEPER EXPLANATION · Learn step by step

1. Weather is what happens over short periods: today's temperature, rain or wind. Climate is the long-term pattern of weather. One unusually cold day does not disprove global warming, just as one very hot day by itself does not establish a long-term climate trend.

2. Sunlight warms Earth. Earth then releases energy as infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit some of that outgoing energy, making the lower atmosphere warmer than it would otherwise be. This natural greenhouse effect is essential for life, but adding extra greenhouse gases strengthens it.

3. Carbon dioxide comes from burning coal, oil and gas and from some land-use changes. Methane comes from sources including livestock, waste and fossil-fuel activities. Nitrous oxide is associated with sources including agriculture. Different gases have different properties and lifetimes.

4. Effects include rising average temperatures, more frequent or intense heat extremes in many regions, shifting rainfall patterns, melting land ice and rising sea level. Agriculture can be affected by heat stress, altered growing seasons, changing water availability and extreme events.

5. Mitigation means reducing the causes of climate change, such as cutting emissions. Adaptation means preparing for its impacts, such as improving drainage, planning for heat, conserving water and developing resilient farming practices. Both are needed.

6. A simple school example: switching off unnecessary lights saves energy; shade trees and cool roofs can help reduce heat exposure; rainwater management can help during heavy rainfall. Different actions address different parts of the problem.

09 · EVS UNIT III

Ozone layer depletion

WHAT? Start with a simple idea

High above us, the stratospheric ozone layer absorbs much of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet radiation. Think of it as a protective filter.

WHY? What causes it or makes it important?

Certain human-made chemicals, including older chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), can release ozone-destroying substances in the stratosphere.

WHAT HAPPENS? Understand the effects

Less protective ozone can increase harmful UV exposure, affecting skin, eyes, plants and some aquatic organisms. Ozone depletion is different from global warming.

HOW CAN WE HELP?

Avoid releasing ozone-depleting substances, safely manage old cooling equipment and support the Montreal Protocol.

Think and explain: Can you give one example of this topic from your home, school, street or city? Explain the cause, one possible effect and one practical solution.

DEEPER EXPLANATION · Learn step by step

1. The atmosphere has several layers. Most weather happens in the troposphere, closest to the ground. Above it is the stratosphere, where much of the protective ozone layer is found. Ozone is a molecule containing three oxygen atoms.

2. Certain manufactured chemicals once used widely in refrigeration, aerosols and other applications can persist long enough to reach the stratosphere. There, sunlight can break them down and release substances that destroy ozone molecules through chemical reactions.

3. The term ozone hole describes severe seasonal thinning of stratospheric ozone, especially over Antarctica. It is not literally an empty hole in the sky. Ozone depletion allows more harmful ultraviolet radiation to reach the surface.

4. The Montreal Protocol is an important example of international environmental cooperation. It has driven the phase-out of many ozone-depleting substances. Proper recovery and disposal of refrigerants from old equipment helps avoid releases.

5. Remember the distinction: ozone high in the stratosphere protects us; ozone near the ground is an air pollutant. Ozone depletion and climate change are separate problems, though some chemicals and policies connect them.

10 · EVS UNIT III

Acid rain

WHAT? Start with a simple idea

Rainwater is naturally slightly acidic. Acid rain means rain or other deposition that is more acidic than normal because of air pollutants.

WHY? What causes it or makes it important?

Sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from fuel burning can react in the atmosphere to form acids that return to land and water.

WHAT HAPPENS? Understand the effects

Acid deposition can harm lakes, forests, soil and some buildings. It may also reduce nutrient availability for plants.

HOW CAN WE HELP?

Reduce sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxide emissions through cleaner fuels, emission controls and energy efficiency.

Think and explain: Can you give one example of this topic from your home, school, street or city? Explain the cause, one possible effect and one practical solution.

DEEPER EXPLANATION · Learn step by step

1. Pure water is neutral at pH 7, but ordinary rain is naturally mildly acidic because carbon dioxide dissolves in it. Acid rain refers to precipitation that becomes more acidic through pollution-related atmospheric chemistry.

2. When sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides are emitted, they can react with water and oxidants in the atmosphere to form sulfuric and nitric acids. These substances may return to Earth in rain, snow, fog or as dry deposition.

3. Acid deposition can change the chemistry of lakes and streams, making conditions unsuitable for some aquatic organisms. It can also affect soil nutrients and damage sensitive forests. Some building stones and metals weather faster in acidic conditions.

4. The most effective prevention is reducing emissions of sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides. Examples include low-sulfur fuels, pollution-control technology, cleaner energy and better combustion systems.

5. Students should distinguish acid rain from a simple dirty-looking rain shower. Its chemistry matters, and effects may appear gradually in soils, lakes and buildings.

11 · EVS UNIT III

Environmental laws in India — why do we need rules?

WHAT? Start with a simple idea

Imagine a classroom with no rules for littering or noise. Shared spaces would become difficult to use. Environmental laws set responsibilities and standards to protect common resources.

WHY? What causes it or makes it important?

The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 provides a broad framework for environmental protection. The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 and Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981 address pollution control.

WHAT HAPPENS? Understand the effects

The Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972 protects wildlife through legal measures. The Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 regulates specified diversion of forest land. The laws have been amended over time, so exact legal requirements should be checked in current official texts.

HOW CAN WE HELP?

Citizens, businesses and public authorities all have roles: follow standards, handle waste responsibly, report serious pollution and protect shared natural resources.

Think and explain: Can you give one example of this topic from your home, school, street or city? Explain the cause, one possible effect and one practical solution.

DEEPER EXPLANATION · Learn step by step

1. Environmental law helps translate the idea of protecting nature into responsibilities, standards and enforcement mechanisms. Different laws address different resources and activities; no single rule solves every environmental problem.

2. The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 established a framework for preventing and controlling water pollution, including pollution control boards. The Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981 provides a framework for controlling air pollution.

3. The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 gives the central government broad powers to take measures for environmental protection. Rules and notifications made under it address specific issues. For legal details, always consult the current official legislation and applicable regulations.

4. The Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972 addresses protection of wild animals, birds and plants, including protected areas and regulated activities. The Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980, subsequently amended and renamed, regulates specified use of forest land for non-forest purposes.

5. A simple example is an industry that plans to release wastewater. The question is not only whether the factory creates jobs; it must also meet applicable environmental requirements and protect people downstream. Responsible development considers both livelihoods and environmental safeguards.

6. For revision, remember the pairing: Water Act → water pollution; Air Act → air pollution; Environment Protection Act → broad environmental framework; wildlife law → species and protected areas; forest conservation law → regulation of forest-land diversion.

12 · EVS UNIT III

International agreements — countries working together

WHAT? Start with a simple idea

Air and water do not stop at national borders. Many environmental problems require countries to cooperate.

WHY? What causes it or makes it important?

The Montreal Protocol addresses ozone-depleting substances. The Kyoto Protocol set emission-reduction commitments for specified developed-country parties. The Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) concerns conservation, sustainable use and fair benefit-sharing.

WHAT HAPPENS? Understand the effects

These agreements show that countries can set shared goals, exchange knowledge and coordinate action, even though their responsibilities and legal mechanisms differ.

HOW CAN WE HELP?

Learn the simple memory link: Montreal → ozone; Kyoto → greenhouse-gas emissions; CBD → biodiversity.

Think and explain: Can you give one example of this topic from your home, school, street or city? Explain the cause, one possible effect and one practical solution.

DEEPER EXPLANATION · Learn step by step

1. An international environmental agreement is a formal arrangement among countries to cooperate on a shared problem. Countries may agree to reduce harmful substances, report progress, exchange technology or protect shared resources.

2. The Montreal Protocol, adopted in 1987, addresses substances that deplete the ozone layer. It is associated with the Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer. Its focus is specific chemicals, not a general ban on all air pollution.

3. The Kyoto Protocol, adopted in 1997 under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, established quantified greenhouse-gas emission commitments for certain developed countries during defined commitment periods. Climate cooperation has continued through other agreements, including the Paris Agreement.

4. The Convention on Biological Diversity, opened for signature in 1992, has three central objectives: conservation of biological diversity; sustainable use of its components; and fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from genetic resources.

5. Compare the problems: ozone-depleting chemicals can affect the protective ozone layer; greenhouse gases alter Earth's energy balance; biodiversity loss reduces the variety of life. The agreements address different causes and require different forms of action.

6. Classroom memory exercise: write three headings — OZONE, CLIMATE, BIODIVERSITY. Place Montreal, Kyoto and CBD beneath the appropriate headings, then explain the reason in one sentence.

Nature reserves, tribal communities and wildlife conflict

WHAT? Start with a simple idea

A nature reserve is an area managed to protect ecosystems and wildlife. Forests are also homes and livelihood spaces for many people, including Adivasi communities.

WHY? What causes it or makes it important?

Protecting nature requires both biodiversity conservation and attention to legitimate community rights. Human–wildlife conflict may occur when farms, roads or settlements overlap with animal movement and feeding areas.

WHAT HAPPENS? Understand the effects

For example, elephants may enter cropland or wild animals may approach settlements. People can lose crops or feel unsafe, while animals may be injured. Communities may also depend on forests for food, livelihoods and culture.

HOW CAN WE HELP?

Support habitat connectivity, early warning, careful land-use planning, fair compensation where applicable, and respectful participation of local and tribal communities.

Think and explain: Can you give one example of this topic from your home, school, street or city? Explain the cause, one possible effect and one practical solution.

DEEPER EXPLANATION · Learn step by step

1. A protected area is a place managed for conservation. Examples of protected-area categories in India include national parks and wildlife sanctuaries. Different legal categories can have different rules and management objectives.

2. Nature conservation does not mean only keeping animals alive. Healthy habitats need water, food, nesting sites, vegetation and space for movement. Wildlife corridors connect habitats and can help animals move between them.

3. Many forest-dwelling and tribal communities have long-standing social, cultural and livelihood relationships with forest landscapes. Conservation decisions should recognize lawful rights and include meaningful participation by affected communities.

4. Human–wildlife conflict is not simply animals behaving badly. It often arises when habitats are fragmented, crops attract wildlife, roads interrupt movement or settlements expand near natural areas. Both human safety and animal welfare need attention.

5. Imagine elephants moving along a traditional route that now crosses farmland. Solutions might involve protecting movement corridors, community alerts, suitable barriers where appropriate and fair responses to crop losses. One solution alone may not work everywhere.

6. A good conservation plan considers ecology, local livelihoods, legal protections, community knowledge and long-term monitoring. Protecting wildlife and respecting people are complementary goals.

14 · REVISION AND CLASSROOM PRACTICE

Check your understanding

Try answering each question in your own words before opening the answer. For longer answers, use the four-part method: meaning, causes, effects and prevention.

Why is clean air important?

Our bodies need air continuously. Harmful particles and gases can damage health and plants.

How can dirty water harm fish?

Pollution may introduce toxic substances or lower dissolved oxygen, making survival difficult.

Why should waste be separated?

Different materials need different treatment; segregation makes recycling, composting and safe disposal easier.

Is ozone depletion the same as global warming?

No. Ozone depletion reduces protection from UV radiation; global warming is rising average surface temperature mainly driven by greenhouse gases.

What is the purpose of the Montreal Protocol?

It coordinates action to phase out ozone-depleting substances.

How can humans reduce conflict with wildlife?

Protect habitats and corridors, use early warnings, plan land use carefully and involve affected communities.

REMEMBER

Healthy air, water, soil, biodiversity and communities are connected. Preventing harm is usually easier than repairing damaged ecosystems.
