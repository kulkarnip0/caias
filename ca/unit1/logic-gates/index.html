05 · DIGITAL LOGIC CIRCUITS

Digital Logic Circuits: from electrical states to computer decisions.

Before learning individual gates, it is important to understand what digital logic actually means. A computer is an electronic system, but it becomes useful because its electrical signals are interpreted as logical information. Digital logic circuits provide the bridge between physical voltages and operations such as deciding, comparing, selecting, counting, storing and calculating.

01 · INTRODUCTION TO DIGITAL LOGIC CIRCUITS

Why do computers use digital logic?

At the electronic level, a processor does not directly understand words such as “yes”, “no”, “true”, “false”, “add”, “compare” or “select”. What it can work with are electrical signals. Digital electronics simplifies those continuously varying electrical quantities into a small number of clearly separated logical states. In ordinary binary digital systems, we use two states and represent them as 0 and 1.

These two symbols are not merely the decimal numbers zero and one. They are labels for two logical conditions. Depending on the context, 0 and 1 may represent LOW/HIGH voltage, OFF/ON, FALSE/TRUE, NO/YES, disabled/enabled, or one of two possible encoded states. The physical voltage ranges depend on the electronic technology, but the logical reasoning remains the same.

Physical voltage→Logic 0 / Logic 1→Boolean operation→Output 0 / 1→Larger digital circuit

The central idea

A digital logic circuit receives one or more binary inputs, performs a precisely defined logical operation, and produces one or more binary outputs. That simple idea scales from a single gate to an entire processor.

What is a logic gate?

A logic gate is the basic decision-making building block of a digital circuit. Each gate follows one specific rule. For example, an AND gate asks whether all required conditions are true, an OR gate asks whether at least one condition is true, a NOT gate reverses a condition, XOR checks whether two inputs are different, and XNOR checks whether they are equal.

The important point is that the gate does not “think” in the human sense. Its behaviour is fixed by its electronic structure. When the inputs change, the output changes according to the gate's Boolean rule.

Why use Boolean algebra?

Boolean algebra gives us a mathematical language for describing logic circuits. Instead of drawing an entire circuit every time, we can write an expression such as Y = A·B for AND or Y = A + B for OR. These symbols do not have exactly the same meaning as ordinary arithmetic. In Boolean algebra, the dot represents logical AND and the plus sign represents logical OR.

This mathematical representation is useful because a digital circuit can be analysed, simplified and redesigned using Boolean laws. A complicated network of gates may sometimes be replaced by a simpler equivalent circuit that produces exactly the same outputs for every possible input combination.

What is a truth table and why do we need it?

A truth table lists every possible combination of input values and shows the corresponding output. It is one of the most reliable ways to understand or verify a logic function because nothing is left unspecified.

What are combinational logic circuits?

When several logic gates are connected together, they form a combinational logic circuit. In a combinational circuit, the present output depends only on the present input values. Examples include adders, subtractors, comparators, multiplexers, demultiplexers, encoders and decoders.

For example, a half adder uses XOR and AND gates together. XOR gives the SUM bit, while AND gives the CARRY bit. This shows why individual gates are important: the gate itself is simple, but useful computing functions appear when gates are connected in meaningful combinations.

Logic Gates→Adders / Comparators / MUX / Decoders→ALU and datapath→Processor

What about circuits that remember previous values?

Not all digital circuits depend only on current inputs. Sequential logic circuits include memory, so their outputs can depend on both present inputs and previous states. Flip-flops, registers and counters belong to this category. In other words, combinational logic performs immediate transformations, while sequential logic adds the idea of state and memory.

Combinational logic

Output depends on the current inputs. Examples: adders, comparators, multiplexers, encoders and decoders.

Sequential logic

Output can depend on current inputs plus previously stored state. Examples: flip-flops, registers and counters.

How do logic gates exist physically?

Logic gates are ultimately built from electronic switching devices such as transistors. Modern integrated circuits contain enormous numbers of transistors arranged so that their collective electrical behaviour implements logic gates and larger digital functions. Therefore the hierarchy can be imagined as:

Transistors→Logic Gates→Functional Circuits→Registers / ALU / Control→CPU

This is why logic gates are studied in Computer Architecture. They explain the lowest logical level from which arithmetic units, control logic, memory structures and ultimately the entire processor are built.

How should you study a gate?

For every gate, answer the same five questions: What condition makes the output 1? What condition makes it 0? What is its Boolean expression? What does its truth table show? Where is this behaviour useful? If these five ideas are clear, there is very little to memorize mechanically.
