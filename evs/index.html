🌿 Ecosystem: Structure and Function

Click each heading below for detailed point-wise content.

What is an ecosystem?
  • An ecosystem is a functional unit where living organisms interact with one another and with their physical environment.
  • It includes both biotic and abiotic components.
  • These components are linked through energy transfer, nutrient cycling and ecological interactions.
  • Examples include ponds, forests, grasslands, deserts, rivers and oceans.
Biotic components
  • Producers: green plants and algae that manufacture food through photosynthesis.
  • Primary consumers: herbivores that feed directly on producers.
  • Secondary/tertiary consumers: carnivores and higher predators.
  • Decomposers: bacteria and fungi that break down dead organic matter and return nutrients to the environment.
Abiotic components
  • Sunlight is the main external energy source for most ecosystems.
  • Water influences metabolism, distribution of organisms and productivity.
  • Air supplies gases such as oxygen and carbon dioxide.
  • Soil provides minerals, water, anchorage and habitat.
  • Temperature, pH, salinity and climate influence which organisms can survive.
Main functions of an ecosystem
  • Energy flow: Sun → producers → consumers → decomposers.
  • Nutrient cycling: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and other nutrients circulate between living and non-living components.
  • Decomposition: dead matter is broken down and nutrients become available again.
  • Ecological regulation: interactions among species help maintain ecosystem balance.
  • Ecosystem services: food, clean water, soil formation, pollination, climate regulation and habitat.

☀️ Energy Flow in an Ecosystem

Energy moves in one direction and progressively less usable energy is available at higher trophic levels.

How does energy enter an ecosystem?
  • Solar energy is captured by producers through photosynthesis.
  • Plants convert light energy into chemical energy stored in food.
  • Herbivores obtain this energy by feeding on producers.
  • Carnivores obtain energy by feeding on other consumers.
  • Decomposers obtain energy from dead organic matter.
Why does energy decrease at higher trophic levels?
  • Organisms use energy for respiration, movement, growth and maintenance.
  • Part of the energy is released as heat at every transfer.
  • Not all biomass is eaten, and not all eaten material is digested.
  • Therefore higher trophic levels receive less usable energy.
  • This is why ecosystems usually support fewer organisms at the top of the food chain.
Ecological pyramids
  • Pyramid of numbers: compares the number of organisms at trophic levels.
  • Pyramid of biomass: compares the mass of living material.
  • Pyramid of energy: compares energy available at successive trophic levels.
  • The energy pyramid is upright because energy is lost during each transfer.

🕸️ Food Chains and Food Webs

Follow the arrows: they show the direction in which energy is transferred.

Food chain
  • A food chain is a simple linear sequence showing who eats whom.
  • It normally begins with a producer.
  • Each feeding position is called a trophic level.
  • Example: Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Hawk.
  • Aquatic example: Phytoplankton → Zooplankton → Small fish → Large fish.
Food web
  • A food web is a network of interconnected food chains.
  • One organism may feed on several species and may itself be eaten by several predators.
  • Food webs represent natural ecosystems more realistically than a single food chain.
  • Alternative feeding links can provide resilience when one food source declines.
  • A major change in one population can affect many other organisms in the web.
Why are arrows important?
  • The arrow points from the organism being eaten to the organism that receives the energy.
  • So grass → rabbit means energy stored in grass moves to the rabbit.
  • Arrows represent energy transfer, not the direction in which an animal moves.

🌱 Ecological Succession

Communities change gradually over time, especially after a new surface forms or a disturbance occurs.

What is ecological succession?
  • Ecological succession is the gradual and orderly change in the species composition of a community over time.
  • Early species modify environmental conditions.
  • Those changes may allow different species to establish later.
  • Succession can increase soil development, biomass and structural complexity.
Primary succession
  • Begins on a surface where soil is initially absent.
  • Examples include fresh lava, newly exposed rock and areas uncovered by retreating glaciers.
  • Lichens and mosses may act as pioneer species.
  • Weathering and organic matter gradually create soil.
  • Typical sequence: bare rock → pioneers → grasses → shrubs → trees.
Secondary succession
  • Occurs after disturbance where soil is still present.
  • Examples include abandoned farmland, many forest fires and flood-disturbed vegetation.
  • Seeds, roots, microorganisms and nutrients may survive.
  • Because soil already exists, recovery is generally faster than primary succession.
